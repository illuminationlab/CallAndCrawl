Starting a pest control business

Starting a pest control business?
Here’s the whole roadmap.

You know the field work cold — you can find the harborage, read the conducive conditions, and close out the account. What nobody handed you is the map for the rest of it: the applicator license, the entity paperwork, the insurance, what to charge, and where the first customers actually come from. This is that map, laid out in the order you will hit it.

Most people who start a pest control company were a tech first. You spent years under houses and up ladders, you got good, and one day you realized you were building someone else’s book of business instead of your own. That is a great reason to go out on your own. It is also the moment the questions start — the ones the truck never taught you. Do I need my own license or can I work under someone else’s? What does insurance actually cost? How much truck and equipment do I really need on day one? What do I charge so I do not starve or scare people off? And once the phone is mine, how do I get it to ring at all? You do not have to have every answer today. You just need the order to tackle them in, and a system that keeps the small stuff from burying you while you are still doing the work yourself.

The roadmap

Six steps from “I want to go out on my own”
to a truck on the road.

01

Applicator licensing, certification & insurance

This is the gate, and it is state-specific. Most states require a structural pest control license and a certified/commercial pesticide applicator credential — you pass a Core exam plus at least one category exam (general household, termite/WDO, fumigation, etc.), usually needing 70 percent or better. Plan on real study time and CEUs to keep it current. Most states also require the business to name a designated certified applicator who supervises the work. Then carry insurance: general liability, professional liability, commercial auto, and workers’ comp if you hire — commonly in the $2,000–$6,000 a year range, with some states also requiring a surety bond or minimum liability limits.

02

Register the business (entity, EIN, permits)

Form the entity most owners choose an LLC for liability protection get your EIN from the IRS, open a business bank account, and register for state and local taxes. On top of the standard paperwork, pest control carries an extra layer: a state pest control / pesticide business license that is separate from your personal applicator certification, plus any local operating permits. Get this stack right before you treat a single account — regulators do check.

03

Vehicle, equipment & startup costs

You can start leaner than you think, but the ranges are real. A dependable used service vehicle runs roughly $15,000–$25,000 (more if you buy new or need a wrap and shelving). Professional-grade equipment — power sprayer, backpack sprayers, dusters, bait guns, a fogger, rodent stations — lands around $2,000–$7,500. Add an initial chemical inventory of roughly $1,000–$2,500 and licensing/insurance up front. All in, a lean solo startup often falls in the $10,000–$50,000 band depending on how much truck and equipment you buy on day one.

04

Set your prices & quote first jobs

Price for margin, not just to win the job. Residential recurring plans commonly sit in the $35–$75 per month range, one-time services price by pest and property, and commercial accounts run wider — often $100–$500 a month by size and complexity. Build a simple, repeatable quote so you are not reinventing the number on every call, know your drive time and product cost per stop, and do not let a lowball competitor set your floor.

05

Get your first customers

New pest control demand is local and search-driven. Claim and fill out your Google Business Profile, then work reviews relentlessly — they are the single biggest lever on local rank and on whether a stranger calls you over the shop next to you. Layer in the rest: door hangers and yard signs in the neighborhoods you treat, referral asks after every job, a simple website with a booking page, and paid channels (direct mail and local search ads typically cost real money per new customer, so track it).

06

The systems that keep a new owner from drowning

In month one you are the technician, the dispatcher, the receptionist, the bookkeeper, and the marketer. The owners who make it are the ones who stop doing the repeatable parts by hand early: answering the phone while under a house, texting missed callers back, sending review requests, chasing invoices, and reminding customers about the next visit.
